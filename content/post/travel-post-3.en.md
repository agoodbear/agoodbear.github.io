This hike up the Paoma Historic Trail in Jiaoxi was our **pre-trip training for Jiaming Lake at the end of June**: heavy packs on, a full day of climbing, to build up leg strength and teamwork first.

I left before dawn and followed the group up the **Paoma Historic Trail**. We walked from Jiaoxi Hot Spring Park in town to Jinmian Daguan at the north entrance, then turned around the way we came. The Apple Watch logged a hiking distance of **12.88 km**, with 500-plus meters of climbing, in nearly seven hours. Along the way there were century-old trees, a mountain-god Earth God shrine, watermelon by the stream, parents playing Little Bee (a roving support car) and handing out popsicles, and a view of Guishan Island from the top. A solid day with a lot of warmth in it.

## Setting Out From Jiaoxi Town

We met at **Jiaoxi Hot Spring Park**. Before setting off we formed a circle for a morning huddle and went over the day's route and safety notes.

Then we shouldered the heavy packs and walked along the town road toward the mountains.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-start.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>Setting out with loaded packs</figcaption></figure>

There is a shortcut from Jiaoxi town onto the Paoma Historic Trail. The key is **the fork on Gongyuan Road near No. 96**. At the junction stands a blue **No Through Road** sign, with a convex corner mirror beside it. Don't let the sign fool you: it means the road is a dead end for **cars**, but **people** can cut in along the small path beside it and join the Fujin Trail entrance to climb up. It's easy to hesitate or walk past because of the sign, so just look for that blue sign and turn in along the small path next to it.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-shortcut.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>The shortcut entrance: when you see No Through Road, you cut in along the side instead</figcaption></figure>

## Climbing All the Way Up

Right after cutting onto the trail, we ran into a wild bee with real character, the **blue-banded bee**.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-bee.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>A blue-banded bee by the trail</figcaption></figure>

> **🐝 Fun fact: the blue-banded bee that sleeps in a hanging group**
>
> What we met by the trail that day was a common wild bee in Taiwan, the **blue-banded bee** (*Amegilla calceifera*). Four to five aqua-blue crossbands on the abdomen shimmer like metal, which is where the name comes from. The most unusual thing happens at dusk: **male** bees with no nest to go back to gather together and clamp onto a dry twig or grass stem with their mandibles for the night, hanging in a whole string. Their mandibles work like small pincers that lock themselves, so once they bite down it takes almost no effort, and they dangle there asleep until morning. Rivals who were fighting over territory in the daytime end up sleeping on the same branch at night.[^bee]

## At the Top: Jinmian Daguan

We reached the **Jinmian Daguan** level, the high point of the north entrance, where a pavilion let us sit down for lunch and catch our breath.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-lunch.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>At the Jinmian Daguan stone marker level, the pavilion ready for lunch</figcaption></figure>

There is a stone marker for the Paoma Historic Trail here, with an eye-catching **little red horse** standing beside it. I took it for just a cute photo-op installation, but looking it up, it turns out to have quite a pedigree.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><img src="/images/paoma/18-red-horse.jpg" alt="The Paoma Historic Trail stone marker and the little red horse"><figcaption>The Paoma Historic Trail stone marker and that little red horse</figcaption></figure>

> **🐴 Fun fact: the little red horse is the emblem of the Olle friendship trails linking Korea, Japan and Taiwan**
>
> That eye-catching little red horse on the trail is more than a photo-op. It is the marker of the **Olle friendship trails**. The Olle trails originated on **Jeju Island in Korea**, and the red horse is modeled on Jeju's native wild pony. Miyagi in Japan learned from Jeju Olle and built Miyagi Olle, and in 2023 it signed an international friendship-trail pairing with Taiwan's **Tamsui-Kavalan Trails**, putting the red-horse marker on Taiwanese mountain trails. Four historic trails in Taiwan are in the program, and the **Paoma Historic Trail** is one of them, paired with Miyagi's **Osaki/Naruko Onsen Trail**. A century-old pack-horse trail now carries one more layer, a trail friendship spanning Korea, Japan and Taiwan: the red horse walked all the way from Jeju's grasslands to the mountains of Jiaoxi.[^horse]

The pavilion at the very top has a wider view, looking down over the whole Lanyang Plain.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-pavilion-view.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>Looking down from the pavilion at the top, the scenery is just lovely</figcaption></figure>

On a clear day you can see **Guishan Island** from here, floating quietly on the sea. There is also an interesting marker on the pavilion floor, reading 125.98 km to Yonaguni Island (Japan). Yonaguni is Japan's westernmost island and the inhabited island closest to Taiwan; on a clear day you can even see Taiwan from it, so this distance marker echoes how close Taiwan and Japan are, one sea apart.

## A Surprise on the Way Back

On the way back, our leader Allen stopped at a wall by the trail and pointed out a clump of fern that looked like a broken umbrella, and gave us a lesson.

<figure class="paoma-fig"><video src="/videos/paoma/paoma-fern.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video><figcaption>On the way back, Allen explains the ferns on the wall by the trail</figcaption></figure>

> **🌿 Fun fact: a living fossil from the age of the dinosaurs, the double-fan fern**
>
> The fern on the wall that looked like a broken umbrella is probably **double-fan fern** (*Dipteris conjugata*). Its round leaf splits in the middle into two fan-shaped halves, left and right, which is what Allen meant by it forming two leaves, and locals simply call it the double-leaf fern. It is an ancient relict: the family Dipteridaceae was spread across the earth well before the Jurassic, **over 200 million years ago**, and today its range has shrunk to a line along the coasts of Asia. In Taiwan it mostly grows on sunny low-elevation slopes and is not common, no wonder Allen stopped to introduce it. It still keeps the primitive look of the ancient ferns, with its forked splitting, and is regarded as a living fossil from the age of the dinosaurs.[^fern]

## Some Takeaways From Training

The most useful thing about this kind of training is feeling how your **gear and body interact** every time you stop to rest. For example, if your left shoulder is sorer than your right, or the other way around, ask yourself whether the pack strap on that side should be looser or tighter. Fine-tuning these during training and finding the fit that suits you will save a lot of effort on the real Jiaming Lake trip.

Another discovery was that **going up and coming down show you completely different scenery**. Take 空ㄟ Farm: I've always thought it was a cool place with beautiful views, and it turns out you pass it on the way up, but I didn't notice it at all going up and only saw it coming down. <!-- keep-zh --> So a reminder to myself: on both the way up and the way down, look around properly instead of just keeping your head down and rushing. Of course, while enjoying the view, watch your footing and stay safe.

One trip up the Paoma Historic Trail took me into the history of a century-old trail, and into the mountain's ecology and its warmth: old trees, an Earth God shrine, watermelon and popsicles, a red horse that walked all the way from Jeju, and a fern from the age of the dinosaurs. There is always something to learn in the mountains.
